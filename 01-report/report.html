Abstract

This report records the results obtained on top of a pre-existing framework, and keeps the two apart. The framework (the base-10 encoding of primes, the end-digit key, the twin segmentation and the colour scheme) is Elizabeth Johnson’s and was published earlier. The results here are new and joint. The main ones are an exact count, 3n+1−2n+13^{n+1}-2^{n+1}, of the admissible residue words of length nn, together with a proof that every word produced by consecutive twin primes is one of them, so that the count is an upper bound for the realised words; a proof that only the prime 55 ever obstructs; the equilibrium obstruction; the exact range of the coprimality test; and a description of the base-10 colour wheel in coordinates, ℤ/10≅ℤ/2×ℤ/5\mathbb Z/10\cong\mathbb Z/2\times\mathbb Z/5, on which every twin pair above 55 avoids the neutral hue. Some supporting facts (digit exclusivity for twin pairs, the uniqueness of 3,5,73,5,7, the carry identity) are classical or elementary and are included because the formalisation needs them. The census, admissibility at 55 of every realised word, the equilibrium obstruction and the wheel identities at W=10W=10 are formalised in Lean 4 with Mathlib, and every axiom footprint is reported.

4 The range of the coprimality test

Theorem 12. Let PP be a product of distinct primes, and let pp be the smallest prime not dividing PP. Let n>1n>1 be an integer.

  1. If gcd(n,P)>1\gcd(n,P)>1 and nn is not itself a prime factor of PP, then nn is composite;

  2. if gcd(n,P)=1\gcd(n,P)=1 and n<p2n<p^2, then nn is prime;

and n=p2n=p^2 is the smallest n>1n>1 with gcd(n,P)=1\gcd(n,P)=1 that is composite.

Proof. (a) A prime factor qq of PP divides nn, and n≠qn\ne q. (b) Every prime below pp divides PP, so every prime factor of nn is at least pp. A composite nn would therefore satisfy n≥p2n\ge p^2. Finally, p2p^2 is coprime to PP and composite, and by (b) nothing smaller is. ◻

The end digit tests coprimality to 1010: nmod10∈{1,3,7,9}n\bmod10\in\{1,3,7,9\} iff gcd(n,10)=1\gcd(n,10)=1. On its own (P=10P=10, p=3p=3) it confirms primality only below 99. Adding the test 3∤n3\nmid n gives the 3030-wheel (P=30P=30, p=7p=7), which confirms primality exactly up to n<49n<49; 49=7249=7^2 is the first failure. Every such finite test therefore refutes reliably but confirms only below p2p^2. Deciding larger nn requires enlarging PP as nn grows, which is trial division.

5 The colour wheel

The colour scheme of the framework labels the ten digits: primaries 1,3,7,91,3,7,9, secondaries 2,4,6,82,4,6,8, grey 55, base 00. This section shows how much of that labelling is intrinsic to the ring ℤ/10\mathbb Z/10 and how much is convention. The orbit partition, the two layers, the hue coordinate and complement as negation are forced. The direction of the hue circle, and which orbit is called primary, are choices. It then places the twin primes on the resulting wheel. The words layer, hue and grey below are names for coordinates. No colorimetric or physical claim is made (Remark 18).

5.3 Twin primes on the wheel

Proposition 17 (twins avoid grey). Let (a,a+2)(a,a+2) be a twin pair with a>5a>5. Both members lie on the unit layer and have non-grey hue. More precisely, h(a)∈{1,2,4}h(a)\in\{1,2,4\} (angles 0,1,20,1,2; digits 1,7,91,7,9) and h(a+2)=h(a)+2∈{3,4,1}h(a+2)=h(a)+2\in\{3,4,1\} (angles 3,2,03,2,0; digits 3,9,13,9,1). The same argument at any odd prime q<aq<a gives amodq∉{0,q−2}a\bmod q\notin\{0,\,q-2\}.

Proof. Both members are primes greater than 55, hence odd and coprime to 55. h(a)≠0h(a)\ne0, and h(a+2)=h(a)+2≠0h(a+2)=h(a)+2\ne0 forces h(a)≠3h(a)\ne3. The general statement is the same argument mod qq. ◻

This is Theorem 8 in wheel coordinates, and it connects the two halves of the report. The twin step x↦x+2x\mapsto x+2 translates the hue additively. Theorem 15 shows that the wheel symmetries are multiplications, which rotate hue. Twin structure therefore lives in the additive structure of the ring and the wheel symmetry in the multiplicative one. The census has the same reading. Every member of a realised tuple has a non-grey hue. Admissibility at 55 says that the offset pattern Δ‾(w)=Δ(w)mod5\bar\Delta(w)=\Delta(w)\bmod5 can be translated off the grey hue: there is an h0∈ℤ/5h_0\in\mathbb Z/5 with 0∉h0+Δ‾(w)0\notin h_0+\bar\Delta(w). This is immediate: take h0=−mh_0=-m for any m∉Δ‾(w)m\notin\bar\Delta(w). Theorem 6 thus counts the words whose hue pattern can be translated off grey. Different words can share a pattern.

Remark 18 (what the wheel does not do). The orbits classify a label system on ℤ/W\mathbb Z/W. Nothing here is a claim about perceived or physical colour, and the words layer, hue and grey are names for coordinates. On prime gaps: a unit uu permutes the residues coprime to WW, so it fixes the set of wheel survivors. We checked by enumeration at W=10,30,210,2310W=10,30,210,2310 that only u=1u=1 preserves the cyclic order of the survivors and only u=−1u=-1 reverses it. For instance, the 3030-wheel gap cycle 6,4,2,4,2,4,6,26,4,2,4,2,4,6,2 is a palindrome up to rotation. The unit multiplications therefore contribute exactly the reflection symmetry of the wheel’s gap pattern. They act on residues only, not on the primes themselves.

5.4 Colour at scale

“Scale” has two meanings here, and the arithmetic treats them differently. Resolution means reading more digits: colouring nn by nmod10kn\bmod 10^k instead of nmod10n\bmod 10. Magnitude means looking at larger numbers. The first is exact. The second is statistical.

Resolution: the tower of wheels. Reduction mod 10k10^k is a ring map π:ℤ/10k+1→ℤ/10k\pi:\mathbb Z/10^{k+1}\to\mathbb Z/10^{k}, the projection from one scale to the next coarser one. By Theorem 13 the colour classes of the 10k10^k-wheel are the gcd classes g=2i5jg=2^i5^j, 0≤i,j≤k0\le i,j\le k, so there are (k+1)2(k+1)^2 of them.

Proposition 19 (classes refine). Projection sends each class of ℤ/10k+1\mathbb Z/10^{k+1} into a single class of ℤ/10k\mathbb Z/10^{k}, namely the one with gcd\gcd equal to gcd(g,10k)\gcd(g,10^k). Over the four classes of the 1010-wheel, the 10k10^k-wheel has 1⏟primaries+k⏟secondaries+k⏟grey+k2⏟base=(k+1)2\underbrace{1}_{\text{primaries}}\;+\;\underbrace{k}_{\text{secondaries}}\;+\; \underbrace{k}_{\text{grey}}\;+\;\underbrace{k^2}_{\text{base}}\;=\;(k+1)^2 classes, the shades of each colour. In particular the primaries never split: nn is a unit mod 10k10^k iff it is a unit mod 1010. Every prime above 55 lies in this single class at every scale.

Proof. gcd(xmod10k,10k)=gcd(x,10k)=gcd(gcd(x,10k+1),10k)\gcd(x\bmod 10^k,10^k)=\gcd(x,10^k)=\gcd(\gcd(x,10^{k+1}),10^k). Iterating down to scale 1010: a class 2i5j2^i5^j lies over the primaries iff i=j=0i=j=0; over the secondaries iff i≥1i\ge1, j=0j=0 (kk choices); over grey iff i=0i=0, j≥1j\ge1 (kk choices); and over the base iff i,j≥1i,j\ge1 (k2k^2 choices). ◻

At scale 100100, for example, the secondaries split into two shades (divisible by 22 only, and by 44). Grey splits into two (divisible by 55 only, and by 2525, i.e. 2525 and 7575). The base splits into four. The hue of the unit class is the coordinate nmod5kn\bmod 5^k. Its circle is the cyclic group (ℤ/5k)×(\mathbb Z/5^k)^\times, which has Nk=4⋅5k−1N_k=4\cdot5^{k-1} elements: 44 hues at scale 1010, 2020 at scale 100100, 100100 at scale 10001000.

Definition 20. A hue circle at scale 10k10^k is the cyclic order x,gx,g2x,…x,gx,g^2x,\dots on (ℤ/5k)×(\mathbb Z/5^k)^\times given by a generator gg; it does not depend on the starting point xx. The circle of hh at scale 10k+110^{k+1} refines the circle of gg at scale 10k10^k if h≡g(mod5k)h\equiv g\pmod{5^k}, equivalently if projection sends each step y↦hyy\mapsto hy to the step π(y)↦gπ(y)\pi(y)\mapsto g\,\pi(y).

Proposition 21 (zooming keeps the coarse order). Let gg generate (ℤ/5k)×(\mathbb Z/5^{k})^\times. Then gmod5g\bmod5 generates (ℤ/5)×(\mathbb Z/5)^\times, so g≡2g\equiv2 or 3(mod5)3\pmod 5. Projected to scale 1010, the circle of gg is the coarse circle of 22, traversed 5k−15^{k-1} times: forwards if g≡2g\equiv2, backwards if g≡3g\equiv3. In particular a circle that refines the coarse circle of 22, in the sense of Definition 20 iterated, runs forwards.

Proof. Reduction (ℤ/5k)×→(ℤ/5)×(\mathbb Z/5^k)^\times\to(\mathbb Z/5)^\times is a surjective homomorphism (every unit mod 55 lifts), so it sends a generator to a generator. The generators mod 55 are 22 and 3=2−13=2^{-1}. If g≡2±1(mod5)g\equiv2^{\pm1}\pmod5, then from any start xx the coarse hue of the θ\theta-th step is x‾2±θ\bar x\,2^{\pm\theta}. That expression advances the coarse angle by ±1\pm1 at every step, and the Nk=4⋅5k−1N_k=4\cdot5^{k-1} steps of the fine circle make 5k−15^{k-1} coarse turns. ◻

Proposition 22 (how much freedom each zoom adds).

  1. At scale 10k10^k there are φ(Nk)\varphi(N_k) hue circles, or φ(Nk)/2\varphi(N_k)/2 up to reflection: 11, 44, 2020 for k=1,2,3k=1,2,3.

  2. A given circle at scale 1010 has exactly 44 refinements at scale 100100. Every circle at scale 10k10^k with k≥2k\ge2 has exactly 55 refinements at scale 10k+110^{k+1}.

  3. The circles of 22 refine one another at every scale: 22 generates (ℤ/5k)×(\mathbb Z/5^k)^\times for every kk.

Proof. (a) The circle of gg determines gg as the successor of 11, and reading the circle backwards gives the circle of g−1≠gg^{-1}\ne g (as Nk>2N_k>2). So there is one circle per generator. (b) The refinements of the circle of gg are the generators mod 5k+15^{k+1} among the five lifts g+j5kg+j5^k. A unit mod pk+1p^{k+1}, with pp an odd prime and k≥2k\ge 2, is a generator iff its reduction mod p2p^2 is [1]. All five lifts have the same reduction mod 2525 as gg, so all five are generators. For k=1k=1: the solutions of x4≡1(mod25)x^4\equiv1\pmod{25} form a subgroup of order 44 with exactly one element over each unit mod 55 (1,7,18,241,7,18,24 over 1,2,3,41,2,3,4). A lift of a generator mod 55 has order 44 or 2020, and it has order 44 exactly when it is that element. So exactly 44 of the 55 lifts generate. For g=2g=2 the exception is 77. (c) 22 has order 2020 mod 2525, and a primitive root mod p2p^2 is a primitive root mod every pkp^k [1]. ◻

At scale 100100 the four refinements of the coarse circle of 22 start, after 11, with 2,4,8,162,4,8,16, or 12,19,3,1112,19,3,11, or 17,14,13,2117,14,13,21, or 22,9,23,622,9,23,6. Each reads the coarse hues 1,2,4,31,2,4,3 five times round. So zooming in preserves the coarse cyclic order exactly once an orientation is fixed, and it still leaves real freedom in the fine order: a finite set of refinements at each step (4 at the first zoom, then 5 at every later zoom). The circle of 22 is a canonical choice that is coherent at every scale.

Two cautions. First, the unit class carries more than its hue: (ℤ/100)×≅C2×C20(\mathbb Z/100)^\times\cong C_2\times C_{20}, where the extra C2C_2 is nmod4n\bmod4. That layer coordinate nmod2kn\bmod2^k refines into (ℤ/2k)×(\mathbb Z/2^k)^\times, which is not cyclic for k≥3k\ge3 ((ℤ/8)×≅C2×C2(\mathbb Z/8)^\times\cong C_2\times C_2). So from scale 10001000 on, the layer side does not form a single circle. Second, hue circles are defined here for the unit class only. How to give hue to the other shades is open.

Magnitude: the colour proportions drift. By the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions, each of the four primaries ends exactly 1/41/4 of the primes asymptotically. At any finite size the proportions are not equal, and the following are measurements over the first 5⋅1065\cdot10^6 primes above 55, not theorems.

  • A persistent lead. The primes ending in 33 or 77 (the non-squares mod 1010) were never strictly behind those ending in 11 or 99 (the squares), checked at every prime. They were tied only at p=11,31,41,3581p=11,31,41,3581. This is Chebyshev’s bias. Under the generalised Riemann hypothesis and a linear-independence hypothesis, Rubinstein and Sarnak [3] showed that such a lead holds on a set of logarithmic density strictly between 00 and 11. So it is expected to fail eventually, on a small but positive proportion of the logarithmic scale: it is persistent, not permanent.

  • An order that churns. Ranking the four digits by count at 6060 logarithmically spaced checkpoints gives 4949 checkpoints with no tie, and the ranking changes 3636 times between consecutive ones. Seven different rankings occur. Checked at every prime, 33 is strictly last in four stretches, starting at p=3571p=3571, 5561955\,619, 5563955\,639 and 5631156\,311.

  • A fading repulsion. The share of consecutive primes with the same last digit was 11.84%11.84\% below 10410^4, 15.35%15.35\% below 10610^6 and 17.12%17.12\% over all 5⋅1065\cdot10^6 primes, against 25%25\% for independent digits. Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan [2] found this bias and gave a conjectural explanation through the Hardy–Littlewood conjectures, in which it decays very slowly, roughly like 1/logx1/\log x. It lives exactly in the sixteen end-digit cells of the framework, which is why no cell count in this work is treated as a constant.

These figures are computed by 04-verification/scale_check.py.

Summary. Under zooming, the coarse cyclic order is preserved exactly, once an orientation is chosen, and each zoom adds a finite, explicitly counted freedom in the fine order (Propositions 21–22). Under growth there is a persistent but not permanent bias, with the finer ranking churning beneath it. The theory of that bias is conditional (Rubinstein–Sarnak) or conjectural (Lemke Oliver–Soundararajan). Both kinds of scale show a coarse structure that persists while the finer order moves, but only the first is a theorem.

5.5 What a theory built on this seed has, and what it still needs

Established here. The wheel ℤ/2×ℤ/p\mathbb Z/2\times\mathbb Z/p has a neutral axis and two layers, each with a circle of p−1p-1 hues on which the units act by rotation, and complement is the half turn. The partition and coordinates are forced by the ring, and the cyclic order of the hues is forced up to the choices in Remark 16. The primes above pp occupy the unit layer. Twin pairs above qq avoid hue 00 and hue −2-2 at every odd prime qq. The census counts the words whose hue pattern can be translated off grey. Across scales, the wheels form a tower in which classes refine and the primaries never split. Each zoom preserves the oriented coarse hue order and adds a finite, counted freedom in the fine order (Section 5.4).

Open, and stated as open.

  • A mixing law. The ring supplies two candidate operations: addition, which adds hues and layers, and multiplication, which rotates hues. Which of them, if either, should model “mixing” is a modelling decision, not a theorem. Under addition, complements mix to the base, as noted above.

  • Finer wheels. Proposition 14 gives one wheel for each odd prime. Every admissible word can be lifted to gap sequences that are admissible at every prime: by the Chinese remainder theorem, choose the gaps ≡0\equiv0 modulo all primes ≥7\ge7 up to any bound. What is open is the converse of Theorem 3, which needs two things. One is a prime kk-tuples statement, that such tuples actually occur. The other is consecutiveness: no further twin pair may fall between the members. The Hardy–Littlewood conjecture alone does not give the second.

  • Rates. Whether the measured equilibrium rates (Section 3) can be expressed through hue statistics is untested.

  • Choosing the order at every scale. Proposition 22 says what the choices are. Whether the framework wants the canonical circle of 22, or some other coherent family, is a modelling decision. So is how to treat the non-cyclic layer side from scale 10001000 on.

  • Shades. Proposition 19 gives the shades of each colour at every scale. Whether shades should carry their own order (for instance by the exponents i,ji,j) is open.

  • Delays beyond 5. The free-hue weights ωq\omega_q at every prime, and the correction for consecutiveness (Section 6), are the natural next quantitative target.

6 Delays on the wheel

A delay is the waiting time until something recurs. This section treats two kinds. The first is the delay, counted in primes, until an end digit comes back. The second is the delay, measured in integers, between twin pairs. The rule for the second is exact; how well it describes the primes is measured, and every measurement below is computed by 04-verification/delay_check.py on the first 5⋅1065\cdot10^6 primes above 55.

6.1 Return delays of a colour

For a sequence in which a colour has frequency ff, the mean delay between its occurrences is 1/f1/f up to boundary terms. This is because the delays sum to the distance from the first occurrence to the last. Each primary has frequency 1/41/4 asymptotically (Section 5.4), so its mean return delay is 44. 8 Scope

  • The converse of Theorem 3 is not claimed. It is observed for n≤5n\le5.

  • No finite test decides primality in general; see Theorem 12.

  • The wheel symmetries return no information about prime gaps (Remark 18).

  • No physical or colorimetric claim is made anywhere in this report.

  • The seed twin starts 33 and 55 are exceptions wherever stated (Remark 4, Theorems 8–11), and every formal statement carries the corresponding hypothesis.
